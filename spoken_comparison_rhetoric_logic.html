Comparison as an Argument Tool

A practical guide to comparing ideas, options, people, risks, costs, evidence, and arguments in spoken professional English. The aim is not to sound academic. The aim is to think sharply and speak naturally.

Foundation

1. Comparison is rarely neutral

The moment you compare one thing with another, you choose a frame. You decide what counts as the benchmark, what feature matters, and what difference deserves attention.

Core rhetorical pattern:
reaction → comparison → reason → conclusion
Yeah, I see why option B looks cheaper. But compared with the cost of rework, the price difference is pretty small. I’d still go with A.

What a comparison can do

Make a choice look better
It’s more expensive upfront, but it’s a lot cheaper to maintain.
Make a criticism look weaker
Sure, it’s late. But we’re talking two days, not two months.
Change the standard
I wouldn’t compare it with last year. The market was completely different.
Expose inconsistency
Why are we treating this case differently from the last one?
Create urgency
The risk of waiting is bigger than the risk of moving now.
Reduce urgency
This is nowhere near as serious as the outage we had in May.
THING A + STANDARD + THING B → DIFFERENCE → SO WHAT? This plan + speed + old plan → faster → launch sooner This plan + cost + old plan → more expensive → maybe not worth it Same object. Different comparison. Different conclusion.

Comparison is usually about one dimension

Good arguments make the dimension clear: cost, speed, risk, fairness, quality, effort, evidence, scale, timing, or relevance.

A: This supplier is better.

B: Better in what way?

A: Mainly reliability. They’re not the cheapest, but they miss fewer deadlines.

Logic check: Never let better, worse, stronger, weaker, bigger, or smaller float without a standard for too long. Ask: better at what?
Control the frame

2. Baselines: the hidden center of comparison

A baseline is the thing you compare against. In argument, whoever controls the baseline often controls the impression.

Same fact, different baseline

FactFrameSpoken version
Project cost is €120kAgainst old budgetYeah, it’s over budget. About twenty grand over.
Project cost is €120kAgainst competitorCompared with what competitors are paying, it’s actually pretty lean.
Project cost is €120kAgainst expected returnIf this really saves half a million a year, 120k isn’t the part I’d worry about.

Useful baseline language

compared with compared to relative to next to against by that standard if you compare it with if the alternative is
Neutral
Compared with Q1, the numbers are better.
More conversational
Yeah, next to Q1, this looks a lot better.
Argumentative
But why are we comparing it with Q1? Q1 was unusually weak.

Attack the baseline, not the conclusion

Often the cleanest attack is not saying the conclusion is wrong. It is showing that the comparison supporting it is badly chosen.

A: Sales are up 30%, so the campaign clearly worked.

B: Compared with what, though? Last month was our worst month all year.

A: Fair. Against the same month last year, it’s more like 8%.

High-value sentence:
I’m not sure that’s the right comparison.
Core spoken structures

3. The comparison structures you actually use

A. More / less / better / worse than

Use these when you want a clear direction.

Defend
It costs more, sure, but it’s also much more reliable.
This is better than pushing something half-finished.
Attack
I don’t see why that option is any better than what we already have.
That creates more risk than it removes.

B. As … as

Use it to show equality, near equality, or challenge exaggerated difference.

The cheaper option is almost as fast.
This issue is just as much about timing as it is about cost.
I’m not saying it’s perfect. I’m saying it’s at least as good as the alternative.

C. Not as … as

Good for calibrated criticism. Less aggressive than saying bad or weak.

The evidence isn’t as strong as it first looks.
I don’t think the difference is as big as we’re making it sound.
It’s not as risky as doing nothing.

D. The same as / different from

That’s not really the same situation.
The principle is the same, even if the numbers are different.
I’d treat this differently. The downside is much bigger here.

E. Similar to / close to / more like

This feels closer to a capacity problem than a motivation problem.
I’d say it’s more like a delay than a failure.
The current situation is pretty similar to what we saw last winter.

F. Rather than / instead of

These compare actions and frame one as the wiser alternative.

I’d fix the process rather than add another approval layer.
Instead of asking whether it’s perfect, ask whether it’s good enough to test.
We should compare the outcomes, not just the upfront costs.

G. Unlike / whereas / while

Useful when two cases need to be separated.

Unlike the old contract, this one locks us in for three years.
The first option saves money, whereas the second saves time.
They solve different problems. One improves speed, while the other improves control.

H. More of X than Y

Extremely useful for reframing what something really is.

This is more of a communication problem than a technical one.
It feels more like a short-term patch than a real solution.
That sounds more like an assumption than evidence.

I. Less about X, more about Y

One of the best spoken reframing structures.

I think this is less about price and more about predictability.
For me, it’s less about who made the mistake and more about how we stop it happening again.
The decision is less about whether we can do it and more about whether we should.

J. Not so much X as Y

Slightly more polished, but still natural in professional speech.

It’s not so much a budget issue as a timing issue.
I’m not so much against the idea as worried about how we’d execute it.

K. As much X as Y

This is as much a trust issue as a process issue.
The delay came from unclear ownership as much as from workload.

L. The more … the more

Use it for relationships and escalating effects.

The longer we wait, the harder this gets to unwind.
The more exceptions we add, the less useful the policy becomes.
The more we optimize for speed, the more careful we need to be about quality.
Strength and distance

4. Show how big the difference really is

Arguments often depend less on whether there is a difference and more on whether the difference is meaningful.

Small difference

a little slightly a bit not much roughly the same
It’s a bit more expensive, but not by much.
The two options are roughly the same on cost.
Performance is slightly better, but I’m not sure it changes the decision.

Large difference

much a lot far way by far nowhere near not even close
The second option is way easier to roll back.
That’s far more expensive than we expected.
The risk isn’t even close. Option B is much safer.
This is nowhere near as disruptive as a full migration.

Professional caution with intensifiers

Way more is natural and common, but it is slightly more casual. In a boardroom, much more or far more may fit better.

CasualNeutral professionalMore formal
way cheapermuch cheapersubstantially less expensive
not even closethere’s a clear gapthe difference is significant
pretty similarfairly similarbroadly comparable
Argument trap: Bigger language can create the illusion of stronger evidence. Saying far more risky does not prove that the gap is actually large.
Rhetorical control

5. Reframing through comparison

Reframing means changing the category, scale, baseline, or alternative so the argument is judged differently.

Move from one category to another

I wouldn’t call this a performance problem. It’s more of a role-clarity problem.
This isn’t really a cost. It’s closer to insurance.
I don’t see this as extra work. I see it as work we’re doing now instead of later.

Change the alternative

A: The redesign is expensive.

B: Compared with doing nothing, sure. Compared with rebuilding this in six months, maybe not.

Change the time horizon

Short term, yes, it’s slower. Over a year, I think it saves us time.
If you only look at this quarter, it looks bad. Over twelve months, the picture changes.

Change the unit of comparison

Per user, the cost is tiny.
Per incident, this is actually cheaper than our current setup.
If you look at cost per successful hire, the agency isn’t that expensive.

Change the object being judged

I’m less concerned about whether the plan sounds ambitious than whether the team can execute it.
The question isn’t whether this is better in theory. It’s whether it works better for us.
Strong spoken move:
That depends what we’re comparing it with.
Reasoning by resemblance

6. Analogy: comparing structures, not just things

An analogy says two situations are similar in a way that matters. It can make an unfamiliar argument feel obvious, but it can also hide important differences.

Simple analogy

Launching without monitoring is a bit like driving without a dashboard. You can do it, but you won’t know something’s wrong until it’s obvious.

Professional analogy

I’d treat this more like a pilot than a full launch. Keep it small, learn, then expand.
Think of the policy as a guardrail, not a wall. It should guide decisions, not block every exception.

Attack an analogy

A: We did this in Germany, so it should work here.

B: Maybe, but how similar are the two markets? The customer mix is pretty different.

Four tests for a useful analogy

  • Relevant similarity: Are the two cases alike on the feature that matters?
  • Relevant difference: Is there a difference that breaks the inference?
  • Scale: Are we comparing a small case with a much bigger one?
  • Mechanism: Does the same cause-and-effect process operate in both cases?
Good attack
I get the analogy. I’m just not sure the part that made it work there exists here.
Defense

7. Defending a position with comparison

Move 1: Compare with the realistic alternative

Yeah, it’s not ideal. But compared with missing the deadline completely, it’s the better option.

Move 2: Admit one weakness, then compare strengths

It is more expensive. I agree. But it’s also faster, safer, and easier to reverse.

Move 3: Shrink the disadvantage

The price difference sounds big in percentage terms, but in actual euros it’s pretty small.

Move 4: Expand the advantage

The benefit isn’t just speed. It gives us much better visibility too.

Move 5: Compare total cost, not sticker price

The license is more expensive, sure. But once you include support and maintenance, the gap gets a lot smaller.

Move 6: Compare risk asymmetrically

If we move now and we’re wrong, we can roll it back. If we wait and we’re wrong, we lose three months. Those aren’t equal risks.

Move 7: Compare standards of evidence

We don’t need certainty. We just need stronger evidence for this option than for the alternatives.

Move 8: Turn perfection into relative improvement

I’m not claiming it fixes everything. I’m saying it’s clearly better than where we are now.
Defense formula:
Yes, X has a weakness. But compared with Y, the weakness matters less than the advantage.

Manager: Why should we approve this if the forecast is uncertain?

You: Because the alternative isn’t certainty. The alternative is making the same decision with less information. This gives us a better basis than we have now.

Attack

8. Attacking an argument with comparison

Move 1: Challenge the comparator

Why are we comparing this with the best-case scenario?
I’m not sure last year is a useful benchmark. Demand was unusually low.

Move 2: Expose missing dimensions

It’s cheaper, yes. But cheaper on price alone. What about downtime and support?

Move 3: Show the cases are not equivalent

Those two situations look similar, but the downside is completely different.
I don’t think that’s an apples-to-apples comparison.

Move 4: Compare the claim with the evidence

That conclusion feels much stronger than the evidence behind it.
We’ve got three examples. That’s not the same as a trend.

Move 5: Compare promise with performance

The pitch sounds good, but how does it compare with what they’ve actually delivered?

Move 6: Compare consistency across cases

We accepted that risk last quarter. What’s different now?
If this standard applies here, shouldn’t it apply to the other team too?

Move 7: Reverse the comparison

A: We shouldn’t change anything because change creates risk.

B: Sure, but staying still creates risk too. Which one is actually bigger?

Move 8: Compare opportunity costs

It may be a good project. I’m just not convinced it’s better than the other things we’d have to stop doing.
Clean attack language:
I’m not saying the comparison is useless. I’m saying it leaves out the part that matters most.
Logic defense

9. Weak and misleading comparisons

False equivalence

Two things share one feature, so someone treats them as equivalent even though important differences remain.

Both options have risk. That doesn’t mean the risks are comparable.

Apples to oranges

The comparison uses objects, periods, groups, or metrics that do not line up.

We’re comparing enterprise customers this year with all customers last year. That’s not really apples to apples.

Cherry-picked benchmark

A favorable or unfavorable reference point is selected while other relevant benchmarks are ignored.

Sure, we beat March. But March was unusually weak. How do we compare with the full-year average?

Percentage without base rate

A 100% increase sounds huge, but what did we go from? Two cases to four?

Different scale

That process worked for a team of five. We have 600 people. I’m not sure it scales the same way.

Different time horizon

It’s cheaper this month. Is it still cheaper over two years?

Different objective

Option A is faster, but speed isn’t our main goal here. Accuracy is.

Survivorship comparison

We keep comparing ourselves with the companies that succeeded. What about the ones that tried the same strategy and failed?

Category mistake

That’s like comparing a policy with a result. One is a rule; the other is an outcome.

Extreme-case comparison

Being better than the worst option doesn’t make this a good option.
Useful rule: A comparison can be factually true and still be rhetorically misleading.
Sophistic awareness

10. Sophistic comparison moves: persuasive, powerful, dangerous

A sophist looks for the frame that makes a case persuasive. Learning these moves helps you argue better, but also helps you notice when someone is manipulating the comparison.

1. Favorable benchmark

Use
Compared with building from scratch, this is cheap.
Counter
True, but is building from scratch actually the alternative?

2. Unfavorable benchmark

Use
We’re still behind the top three competitors.
Counter
Sure, but they’re five times our size. How do we compare with firms at our stage?

3. Contrast amplification

Put two options side by side and exaggerate the practical difference.

One option gives us control. The other leaves us hoping nothing goes wrong.
Powerful because it compresses complexity into a sharp contrast. Dangerous because reality may be less binary.

4. Category relabeling

I wouldn’t call it a delay. I’d call it avoiding rework.
That’s not extra cost. It’s the price of reducing uncertainty.

5. Relative victory

Do not prove something is good. Prove it is better than the alternatives.

Is it perfect? No. Is it better than every realistic option we have right now? I think so.

6. Relative defeat

Do not prove an idea is terrible. Show there is a clearly better use of resources.

I don’t hate the idea. I just think there are better things to spend the next six weeks on.

7. Scale switch

At company level, the cost looks large. Per customer, it’s tiny.
One incident doesn’t look serious. Across 40 teams, it becomes a pattern.

8. Time switch

Today it looks slower. Six months from now, it may be the fastest route.

9. Moral comparison

We’re asking the junior team to meet a standard we don’t apply to leadership.

This can be very persuasive because fairness is naturally comparative.

10. Burden comparison

Which is the bigger burden: documenting the decision now, or explaining a bad decision later?
Ethical discipline: Use the strongest fair comparison, not merely the comparison that produces the strongest emotional effect.
Interrogation by comparison

11. Questions that force the argument into the open

Comparison questions are excellent because they sound curious while testing the logic underneath.

Benchmark questions

Compared with what?
What are we using as the baseline here?
Why are we comparing it with last quarter?
Would the conclusion change if we used the annual average instead?

Dimension questions

Better in what sense?
Are we optimizing for cost or speed?
Which difference matters most here?

Equivalence questions

Are those really the same kind of case?
What makes those two situations comparable?
What’s different enough that the analogy might break?

Magnitude questions

How big is the gap in actual numbers?
Is that a meaningful difference or just a measurable one?
Are we talking slightly worse or materially worse?

Alternative questions

Better than doing what?
What happens if we don’t do this?
Is there a third option we should compare both of these against?

Consistency questions

Would we use the same standard in the opposite case?
Why is this acceptable here but not there?
If the teams were reversed, would we still see it the same way?
Rhetorical advantage: A good comparison question makes the other person supply the missing premise themselves.
Professional use

12. Comparison in real workplace situations

Meetings: choosing between options

A: Which route do you prefer?

You: Probably B. It’s a little slower to set up, but it’s much easier to maintain. Over a year, I think that matters more.

Budget discussions

Finance: This is 15% above the cheaper vendor.

You: Right, on the contract price. But once we include migration and support, the difference is closer to 4%. That feels like a different decision.

Negotiation

Client: Your rate is higher than the other offer.

You: It is. The difference is that our rate includes implementation and support. If you compare the full scope, the gap is a lot smaller.

Performance review

Manager: Your output was lower this quarter.

You: That’s true on volume. On complexity, though, the work was much heavier. I’d look at both before we judge the quarter.

Disagreeing with a senior person

I see the comparison. I’m just not sure the two situations are close enough for us to draw the same conclusion.
That makes sense if speed is the main criterion. I’m not sure it is in this case.

Project prioritization

I like the project. I just don’t think it’s as urgent as the reliability work.
This may create more value eventually, but the other project removes a much more immediate risk.

Client pushback

I understand why that feels expensive. Compared with a standard license, it is. Compared with the cost of a failed rollout, it’s a different picture.

Hiring

Candidate A has more direct experience. Candidate B seems stronger on learning speed and stakeholder management. It depends which gap we’re actually hiring to fill.

Conflict

I don’t think this is about who worked harder. I think it’s more about whether expectations were clear on both sides.

Strategy

The smaller market is easier to enter, but the larger one gives us a much better long-term position.

Interview answers

Compared with my previous role, this one gave me much more ownership. I wasn’t just executing decisions; I was helping shape them.
Sound like a person

13. Rhythm: how comparison sounds in spoken English

The logic can be sophisticated while the sentence remains loose and conversational.

Pattern: short reaction → contrast → conclusion

Yeah, maybe. It is cheaper. I just don’t think it’s cheaper enough to justify the extra risk.

Pattern: concession → comparison → choice

Sure, A is faster. But B is much easier to recover from if something goes wrong. I’d take B.

Pattern: correction mid-sentence

It’s worse—well, worse on speed. On quality, it’s actually better.

Pattern: question first

Compared with what, though? If we’re comparing it with doing nothing, then yeah, it’s expensive.

Pattern: compressed ending

Same cost. Less risk. Easier rollout. So for me, pretty clear.

Pattern: softer professional disagreement

I see why you’d compare those two. I’m just not sure the conditions are similar enough.

Written versus spoken

Written-ishNatural spoken
Option A is preferable because its long-term maintenance costs are lower than those of Option B.I’d go with A. It’s just cheaper to maintain over time.
The two situations are insufficiently comparable to justify the conclusion.I don’t think those cases are close enough to support that conclusion.
The primary issue is not cost but implementation risk.For me, it’s less about cost and more about execution risk.
Precision without stiffness

14. Repairs, softening, and calibration

When your first comparison is too strong

It’s much better—actually, maybe not much better. But definitely easier to use.
They’re basically the same. Well, same on price. The service model is different.

When you want to disagree without sounding combative

I’m not sure I’d compare those directly.
I see the similarity. I think the difference is the scale.
That’s fair. I just think the downside is bigger in this case.

When you are uncertain

It looks slightly better, but I wouldn’t say the gap is clear yet.
My sense is that B is safer, though we probably need the numbers to confirm that.

When you want to sharpen

No, I think the difference is bigger than that.
I’d go further. It’s not just cheaper; it removes an entire failure point.
Useful spoken sequence:
I agree on X. I’m just not sure X matters more than Y.
Practice

15. Drills: turn comparison into argument

Drill 1: Change the baseline

Start with: The new system is expensive.

Possible answers
Compared with the old license, yes.
Compared with the hours we waste now, not really.
If the alternative is hiring two more people, it’s actually cheap.

Drill 2: Change the dimension

Start with: Vendor A is better.

Possible answers
Better on price, yes. Not on support.
They’re faster, but B is more reliable.
It depends what we mean by better.

Drill 3: Defend the weaker-looking option

Option B costs more and takes longer to implement.

Possible answer
True. But it’s also much easier to scale and much safer to roll back. If we’re choosing for the next three years rather than the next three weeks, B makes more sense.

Drill 4: Attack a bad analogy

Someone says: We used this process in a five-person startup, so it should work in a 2,000-person company.

Possible answer
I get the logic, but I’m not sure the situations are comparable. The coordination cost at 2,000 people is completely different.

Drill 5: Make a relative argument

Do not prove your option is perfect. Prove it is better than the realistic alternatives.

Possible answer
I’m not saying this is ideal. I’m saying it gives us more control than waiting and less disruption than a full rebuild.

Drill 6: Expose the hidden standard

Someone says: The team is underperforming.

Questions to ask
Compared with the target or compared with last year?
On output, quality, or both?
Are we comparing teams with similar workloads?

Drill 7: Sophistic defense

Defend a delayed launch without pretending delay is good.

Possible answer
Yeah, the delay hurts. But two extra weeks now is still better than launching unstable and spending two months cleaning it up.

Drill 8: Sophistic attack

Attack a proposal that saves money but increases operational risk.

Possible answer
It’s cheaper on paper. I’m not sure it’s cheaper once you price in the failure risk. We may be comparing invoice cost with total cost.
Fast retrieval

16. Phrase bank: comparison for argument

To compare neutrally

  • Compared with last year, we’re in a better place.
  • Relative to the other options, this is pretty simple.
  • The two are similar on cost but very different on risk.
  • They’re roughly the same in terms of effort.
  • This is closer to X than Y.

To defend

  • It may be more expensive, but it’s also much safer.
  • I’m not saying it’s perfect. I’m saying it’s better than the alternative.
  • The gap is smaller than it looks.
  • Once you include X, the comparison changes.
  • Short term, yes. Long term, I’m not so sure.
  • Compared with the cost of waiting, this is manageable.
  • At least this gives us more control.

To attack

  • Compared with what?
  • I’m not sure that’s the right comparison.
  • Those aren’t really the same kind of case.
  • That feels like an apples-to-oranges comparison.
  • Better in what sense?
  • That conclusion is stronger than the evidence.
  • We’re comparing a best case with an average case.
  • Are we using the same standard on both sides?
  • That’s true on price. I’m not sure it’s true on total cost.

To reframe

  • For me, it’s less about X and more about Y.
  • I’d see this more as X than Y.
  • This isn’t so much a cost issue as a timing issue.
  • I wouldn’t compare it with X. I’d compare it with Y.
  • The real alternative isn’t X. It’s Y.
  • That depends what time horizon we’re using.
  • Per customer, the picture looks very different.

To expose magnitude

  • How big is the difference in practice?
  • Is that materially better or just slightly better?
  • Are we talking 2% or 20%?
  • That sounds big in percentage terms. What is it in absolute numbers?
  • The direction is clear. I’m less sure the size is.

To compare risks

  • Which risk is easier to recover from?
  • The downside of A is much easier to contain.
  • Those risks aren’t symmetrical.
  • The risk of waiting may be bigger than the risk of acting.
  • One failure is reversible. The other isn’t.

To compare evidence

  • The evidence for A is stronger than the evidence for B.
  • We’ve got more examples, but not necessarily better evidence.
  • That analogy is suggestive, not conclusive.
  • The claim is much broader than the data.
  • I’d want a closer comparison before drawing that conclusion.

To sound natural under pressure

  • Yeah, but is it actually better?
  • Maybe. I just think the gap is smaller than that.
  • Same problem, different scale.
  • Similar, yes. The same? Not really.
  • Cheaper, sure. Better? I’m not convinced.
  • That’s fair. I still think the downside is bigger.
  • I get the comparison. I’m not sure it holds.

The compact master template

1. React Yeah, I see that. 2. Name the comparison Compared with X, Y is stronger on Z. 3. Test the standard The question is whether Z is what matters most. 4. Add the missing dimension On cost, yes. On risk, not really. 5. Conclude So I’d still go with Y.
Final principle:
Strong comparison is not just saying one thing is better than another. It is choosing the right baseline, the right dimension, and the right scale — then making the listener feel why the difference matters.
