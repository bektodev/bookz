Natural Lexical Chunks

A practical ebook of ready-made combinations that help you speak faster, more naturally, and with less mental construction.

The principle

Don’t build every sentence from zero. Reach for a useful chunk, then attach the detail you need. Reaction → position → reason works especially well in real conversation.

How to use this guide

  • Learn the whole expression, not the individual words.
  • Say the example out loud once, then replace one detail with your own.
  • Prefer short chunks you can reuse in many situations.
  • Notice how uncertainty is calibrated: could be, probably, I’m not so sure.
  • In technical talk, keep the engineering precise but let the sentence around it stay conversational.

01

Core reaction chunks

Fast responses that let you react first and explain second.

Yeah, exactly.
Strong, natural agreement.
ExampleYeah, exactly. That’s the part I was worried about.
That’s fair.
Accepting that the other person has a reasonable point.
ExampleThat’s fair. I still think we should check the numbers first.
I get what you mean.
Showing that you understand the point, even if you may disagree.
ExampleI get what you mean. I’m just not sure it’ll work in practice.
I see your point.
Acknowledging the logic behind someone’s view.
ExampleI see your point. The timing still feels a little tight, though.
Maybe, yeah.
Soft agreement when you are not fully convinced.
ExampleMaybe, yeah. We could try it once and see how it goes.
Could be.
Saying something is possible without committing to it.
ExampleCould be. I’d still rule out the simpler causes first.
Not really.
A short, natural way to disagree or say something is not true.
ExampleNot really. The issue isn’t the cost. It’s the lead time.
Probably not.
A calibrated negative answer.
ExampleProbably not. We don’t have enough data yet.
I’m not so sure.
Gentle disagreement or doubt.
ExampleI’m not so sure. That assumes the load stays constant.
That makes sense.
Showing that an explanation or decision is logical.
ExampleThat makes sense. If the supplier changed the material, that could explain it.
Right, but…
Agreeing with one part before raising an objection.
ExampleRight, but if we change the shaft, we’ll have to check the bearing fit too.
Okay, so…
Moving from what was said to the next logical point.
ExampleOkay, so we know the motor is fine. What does that leave us with?
02

Keeping the conversation moving

Ready-made chunks for turn-taking, thinking aloud, changing direction, and wrapping up.

I mean…
A natural way to explain, soften, or correct what you just said.
ExampleI mean, it works. I just wouldn’t call it reliable yet.
The thing is…
Introducing the main difficulty or consideration.
ExampleThe thing is, we can’t test it properly until the new sensor arrives.
Here’s the thing.
Bringing attention to the key point.
ExampleHere’s the thing. If we rush this, we’ll probably do the work twice.
Let me think.
Buying a little time while you work something out.
ExampleLet me think. We tested that version on Tuesday, right?
Actually, no.
Correcting yourself naturally.
ExampleActually, no. That was the older drawing.
Wait, sorry.
Stopping briefly because you need to correct or clarify something.
ExampleWait, sorry. Are you talking about the inlet side or the outlet side?
Anyway…
Returning to the main topic after a side point.
ExampleAnyway, the main question is whether we can ship on Friday.
So, basically…
Giving the simple version of a longer point.
ExampleSo, basically, the part fits, but the tolerance is too close for comfort.
Long story short…
Giving only the important outcome of a longer story.
ExampleLong story short, they can make it, but not by next week.
For now…
Limiting a decision to the present situation.
ExampleFor now, I’d leave the setup as it is.
At this point…
Referring to the situation as it currently stands.
ExampleAt this point, replacing the seal is probably faster than troubleshooting it again.
That’s about it.
Natural way to signal that there is nothing important left to add.
ExampleWe checked the alignment, changed the belt, and ran it again. That’s about it.
03

Agreement, disagreement, and pushback

Useful chunks for challenging ideas without sounding stiff or aggressive.

I’m with you on that.
Clear agreement with a specific point.
ExampleI’m with you on that. We shouldn’t change two things at once.
I’d agree with that.
Measured agreement, common in work discussions.
ExampleI’d agree with that. The first test should be the simplest one.
I’m not against it.
Saying you are open to an idea but may still have concerns.
ExampleI’m not against it. I just want to know what it does to the schedule.
I wouldn’t go that far.
Saying someone’s conclusion is stronger than the evidence supports.
ExampleI wouldn’t go that far. One bad test doesn’t mean the design is wrong.
I don’t know about that.
Natural, mild skepticism.
ExampleI don’t know about that. We’re assuming the sensor is accurate.
That doesn’t necessarily mean…
Separating a fact from a conclusion that does not automatically follow.
ExampleThe temperature went up, but that doesn’t necessarily mean the bearing is failing.
I’m not sure that follows.
Questioning the logic between two claims.
ExampleI’m not sure that follows. Lower cost doesn’t automatically mean lower quality.
I’d look at it differently.
Disagreeing by offering another framing.
ExampleI’d look at it differently. The problem isn’t speed; it’s consistency.
I think we’re mixing two things.
Separating issues that are being treated as one.
ExampleI think we’re mixing two things. The design works; the assembly process is the problem.
That’s part of it.
Accepting one cause while implying there are others.
ExampleYeah, that’s part of it. But the setup time is hurting us too.
That’s not quite the issue.
Redirecting attention to the real problem.
ExampleThat’s not quite the issue. The question is whether it stays stable under load.
I’d be careful with that.
Warning against a conclusion, decision, or action.
ExampleI’d be careful with that. We only tested one sample.
04

Clarifying and getting the point

Chunks for making people define what they mean and for avoiding misunderstandings.

What do you mean by that?
Asking someone to define or explain a point.
ExampleWhat do you mean by that? Too expensive compared with what?
Do you mean…?
Checking whether you understood correctly.
ExampleDo you mean the whole assembly, or just the bracket?
Are you saying…?
Testing your interpretation of someone’s claim.
ExampleAre you saying the pump is undersized, or just that the current setting is wrong?
Just to be clear…
Making an important point explicit before continuing.
ExampleJust to be clear, we’re changing the material, not the geometry.
Just so I understand…
Polite way to check the logic or sequence.
ExampleJust so I understand, we test it here first and then send it to the customer?
What exactly is the problem?
Asking for a precise description rather than a vague complaint.
ExampleWhat exactly is the problem? Is it noise, vibration, or loss of pressure?
Which part do you mean?
Narrowing down a vague reference.
ExampleWhich part do you mean? The outer housing or the inner sleeve?
How do you know?
Asking for the basis of a claim.
ExampleHow do you know it’s the seal and not the surface finish?
What are we basing that on?
Asking what evidence supports a conclusion.
ExampleWhat are we basing that on? One test or the full batch?
Can you walk me through it?
Asking someone to explain a process step by step.
ExampleCan you walk me through it? I want to see where the pressure starts to drop.
What am I missing?
Inviting correction when something does not add up.
ExampleWhat am I missing? If the dimensions are the same, why does this one bind?
So the main point is…?
Checking the core conclusion.
ExampleSo the main point is we can use the old fixture, but we need a new insert?

Time and sequence

Natural chunks for talking about timing, order, duration, delay, and deadlines.

for the time being
For now; until the situation changes.
ExampleFor the time being, let’s keep the old setup.
sooner or later
At some point in the future; eventually.
ExampleSooner or later, we’ll have to replace that machine.
from time to time
Occasionally.
ExampleIt makes that noise from time to time, but not on every cycle.
every now and then
Occasionally, in an informal way.
ExampleI still check the old drawings every now and then.
right away
Immediately.
ExampleIf the pressure drops again, shut it down right away.
for a while
For a period of time.
ExampleLet it run for a while and see if the temperature settles.
by then
No later than that future point.
ExampleThe new bearings should be here by then.
up to now
From the past until the present.
ExampleUp to now, we haven’t seen the same failure twice.
from now on
Starting now and continuing into the future.
ExampleFrom now on, let’s record the torque for every unit.
one at a time
Individually, not all together.
ExampleChange one thing at a time, or we won’t know what fixed it.
step by step
In a clear sequence.
ExampleLet’s go through it step by step.
at the same time
During the same period or while another thing is happening.
ExampleWe can’t inspect the part and machine it at the same time.
ahead of time
Before something is needed or scheduled.
ExampleSend me the dimensions ahead of time and I’ll check the fit.
at the last minute
Very close to the deadline.
ExampleI’d rather not change the drawing at the last minute.
for the first time
On the first occasion something happens.
ExampleFor the first time, the temperature stayed stable through the whole test.

out of the way
Not blocking movement or work.
ExampleCan we move these boxes out of the way?
in the middle of
At the center of something or during an activity.
ExampleWe’re in the middle of a test, so give us ten minutes.
next to
Immediately beside something.
ExampleThe pressure sensor is next to the inlet valve.
close to
Near a place or near a value.
ExampleKeep the cable close to the frame.
away from
At a distance from something.
ExampleKeep your hands away from the rotating shaft.
back and forth
Repeatedly in two opposite directions.
ExampleThe arm moves back and forth about twenty times a minute.
up and down
Repeatedly in a vertical direction.
ExampleThe reading keeps going up and down.
in place
In the correct position.
ExampleHold the bracket in place while I tighten the bolts.
out of position
Not where something should be.
ExampleThe seal was slightly out of position.
all the way
To the full distance or limit.
ExamplePush it all the way in.
side by side
Next to each other.
ExamplePut the two samples side by side and compare the wear.
around the corner
Very close in distance, or sometimes in time.
ExampleThe machine shop is just around the corner.

Chunks for saying how well, how much, or in what way something happens.

A small amount.
ExampleMove it a little bit to the left.
quite a bit
A fairly large amount.
ExampleThe new version is quite a bit quieter.
not much
Only a small amount.
ExampleThere’s not much difference between the two readings.

Approximately; almost.
ExampleThe dimensions are more or less the same.
pretty much
Almost completely or generally.
ExampleThat’s pretty much what happened.
by far
Clearly more than all the others.
ExampleThis is by far the simplest option.
good enough
Acceptable for the purpose, even if not perfect.
ExampleThe finish isn’t perfect, but it’s good enough for the prototype.
close enough
Near enough to the target or requirement.

well within
Comfortably inside a limit.

08

Plans, choices, and decisions

Conversational chunks for proposing, choosing, delaying, and committing.

I’d go with…
Natural way to state your preferred option.
ExampleI’d go with the smaller motor. We don’t need the extra power.
I’d rather…
Expressing a preference.
ExampleI’d rather test it once more before we release the drawing.
We might as well…
Doing something because there is little reason not to.
ExampleWe might as well change the seal while the pump is already open.
Let’s stick with…
Continue using the current choice.
ExampleLet’s stick with the original material for now.
Let’s leave it for now.
Delay action until later.
ExampleThe reading is stable again. Let’s leave it for now.
Let’s see how it goes.
Try something and evaluate the result later.
ExampleRun the new setting today and let’s see how it goes.
We can always…
Pointing out a later fallback option.
ExampleWe can always change the bracket if the first version flexes too much.
I don’t see a reason to…
Saying there is no clear justification for an action.
ExampleI don’t see a reason to redesign the whole assembly.
There’s no point in…
Saying an action would not produce useful value.
ExampleThere’s no point in testing it before the adhesive cures.
It depends on…
Showing that the answer changes according to a condition.
ExampleIt depends on how much load the customer actually sees.
If it were up to me…
Giving your own choice while recognizing others may decide.
ExampleIf it were up to me, I’d keep the tolerance a little wider.
I’m fine with that.
Accepting a plan or decision.
ExampleTuesday morning? Yeah, I’m fine with that.
That works for me.
Agreeing that a plan suits you.
ExampleWe can review it after lunch. That works for me.
Let’s call it.
Deciding to stop an activity, usually because enough has been done.
ExampleWe’ve run six tests and the result is the same. Let’s call it.
Let’s take another look.
Re-examine something before deciding.
ExampleThe numbers don’t quite match. Let’s take another look.
09

Problems, struggle, and challenge

Plain, widely understood expressions for difficulty, uncertainty, and failure.

I’m having trouble with…
Saying something is difficult for you.
ExampleI’m having trouble with this connection. It keeps dropping.
I can’t seem to…
Saying repeated attempts are not working.
ExampleI can’t seem to get the two holes to line up.
It keeps happening.
A problem repeats again and again.
ExampleWe reset it, but the same fault keeps happening.
It’s getting worse.
The condition is deteriorating.
ExampleThe vibration is getting worse as the speed goes up.
It’s not getting any better.
There is no improvement.
ExampleWe changed the setting, but the noise isn’t getting any better.
We’re running into…
Encountering a problem during work.
ExampleWe’re running into the same clearance issue again.
We’re struggling with…
Having ongoing difficulty with something.
ExampleWe’re struggling with repeatability on this operation.
The hard part is…
Identifying the main difficulty.
ExampleThe hard part is holding the part without distorting it.
That’s the tricky part.
Calling attention to the part that needs care or judgment.
ExampleGetting it apart is easy. Putting it back in alignment—that’s the tricky part.
It’s a bit of a problem.
Soft way to say something is causing difficulty.
ExampleThe lead time is a bit of a problem.
We’re not there yet.
Saying the result is not ready or good enough yet.
ExampleThe concept works, but we’re not there yet.
It’s not holding up.
Something is failing to remain strong, stable, or effective.
ExampleThe coating looks fine at first, but it’s not holding up in the wear test.
We need to figure out…
Saying a problem still needs an explanation or solution.
ExampleWe need to figure out why the seal keeps wearing on one side.
Something’s off.
An informal way to say something seems wrong.
ExampleSomething’s off. These two readings shouldn’t be that far apart.
That doesn’t look right.
Natural way to flag a possible problem.
ExampleThat doesn’t look right. The shaft shouldn’t move that much.

Interpersonal friction and difficult conversations

Chunks for disagreement, boundaries, tension, and repair without sounding dramatic.

I don’t want to make a big deal out of it.
Raising an issue while signaling that you want to stay reasonable.
ExampleI don’t want to make a big deal out of it, but this keeps getting changed without telling us.
I’d rather we…
Polite way to state how you want a shared situation handled.
ExampleI’d rather we agree on the dimensions before the next revision.
I’m a little concerned about…
Professional, calm way to raise a worry.
ExampleI’m a little concerned about the amount of play in that joint.
That puts us in a difficult spot.
Explaining that someone’s action creates a practical problem for your side.
ExampleChanging the date now puts us in a difficult spot.
I need a little more notice.
Asking for earlier communication in the future.
ExampleI can help, but I need a little more notice next time.
That’s not what we agreed.
Pointing out a difference from a previous agreement.
ExampleThat’s not what we agreed. We said the change would wait until the next batch.
I think there’s been a misunderstanding.
Soft way to address conflicting expectations.
ExampleI think there’s been a misunderstanding. We weren’t planning to supply the sensor.
Let’s not get stuck on that.
Moving past a side issue that is blocking progress.
ExampleWe both know the drawing needs work. Let’s not get stuck on who changed it first.
Can we keep this practical?
Redirecting a tense or abstract discussion toward useful action.
ExampleCan we keep this practical? What do we need to do before Friday?
I’m not blaming anyone.
Separating problem-solving from personal accusation.
ExampleI’m not blaming anyone. I just want to understand how the wrong part got installed.
Let’s sort it out.
Suggesting that both sides work through a problem.
ExampleThere’s clearly some confusion. Let’s sort it out before the next meeting.
We’re on the same side here.
Reminding people that they share the same broader goal.
ExampleWe’re on the same side here. We all want the machine to run safely.
I can live with that.
Accepting a compromise that is not your ideal choice.
ExampleIf we can get it by Wednesday, I can live with that.
That’s not ideal, but…
Accepting an imperfect situation before moving to a practical response.
ExampleThat’s not ideal, but we can work around it for one build.
Let’s leave it there.
Ending a disagreement or topic without continuing it.
ExampleWe’re not going to agree on that today. Let’s leave it there.

15

Cause, effect, evidence, and comparison

Conversational building blocks for explaining why something happened and comparing options.

That’s because…
Giving a direct reason.
ExampleThe reading jumps at startup. That’s because the valve opens too quickly.
That’s why…
Connecting a cause to its result.
ExampleThe seal was dry. That’s why it wore so fast.
It comes down to…
Reducing a situation to its main deciding factor.
ExampleAt this point, it comes down to cost versus lead time.
The main reason is…
Naming the strongest cause.
ExampleThe main reason is that we don’t have enough clearance.
One reason could be…
Offering a possible cause without claiming certainty.
ExampleOne reason could be that the surface is too rough.
That would explain…
Showing how one fact could account for another.
ExampleIf the coupling was loose, that would explain the vibration.
That doesn’t explain…
Pointing out that a theory leaves evidence unresolved.
ExampleOkay, but that doesn’t explain why it only happens when it’s warm.
Compared with…
Putting one thing against another for evaluation.
ExampleCompared with the old design, this one is easier to assemble.
There’s not much difference.
Saying two options are very similar.
ExampleThere’s not much difference in cost, so I’d choose the easier one to machine.
The big difference is…
Highlighting the most important contrast.
ExampleThe big difference is stiffness. Everything else is pretty close.
The same goes for…
Extending the same reasoning to another case.
