The main rule: keep the thinking organized, but let the sentence sound loose. In professional conversation, clear logic usually sounds better when it comes out in short units: Yeah, I see that. I’m just not sure it gets us there. Maybe we test it first.
Chapter 01

What counts as professional colloquial English

Core idea

Sound like a real person, not a memo.

NaturalYeah, I get the concern. I just don’t think that’s the main issue.
Too writtenWhile I acknowledge the concern, I do not believe it constitutes the primary issue.
Too casualNah, that’s not really the thing.

Rhythm

A useful default rhythm is: reaction → position → reason → short conclusion.

ExampleYeah, maybe. I’m just not sure we need to change the whole process for one bad week. I’d leave it for now.

Logic underneath

You can still reason carefully without naming premise, inference, or conclusion.

ExampleWe’ve only tested it with five people. So I wouldn’t read too much into the result yet.
Chapter 02

Take: one of the most useful professional verbs

take it from here

I’ll handle the next step.

ExampleThanks. I’ve got enough context now. I can take it from here.
LogicResponsibility transfers to the speaker.

take it up with someone

Raise the issue directly with the relevant person.

ExampleThat sounds like a payroll issue. I’d take it up with Finance.
ToneNeutral, common, direct.

take it on board

Accept and seriously consider feedback.

ExampleThat’s fair. I’ll take that on board and rework the opening.
LogicAcknowledgment does not automatically mean agreement.

take it into account

Include something in your judgment.

ExampleWe should take the timing into account before we commit.
UseSlightly more formal, but very natural in meetings.

take someone through something

Explain something step by step.

ExampleGive me five minutes and I’ll take you through the numbers.
Alsowalk you through is even more conversational.

take a look

Review briefly.

ExampleCan you take a quick look before I send it?
ToneNatural request; softer than review this.

take a step back

Reconsider from a wider perspective.

ExampleMaybe we’re getting too deep into the details. Can we take a step back for a second?
RhetoricUseful for redirecting a discussion without directly rejecting anyone.

take the point

Acknowledge the logic of what someone said.

ExampleI take the point. I’m just not sure the same rule applies here.
LogicAgree with one premise while resisting the conclusion.

take your point

A more personal, conversational version of take the point.

ExampleYeah, I take your point. I still think the risk is pretty small.

take something as a given

Treat something as already established.

ExampleI wouldn’t take demand as a given. We still need to test it.
LogicChallenges an unstated assumption.

take something off someone’s plate

Remove work from someone’s workload.

ExampleI can take the client update off your plate this week.
UseVery common and professional-safe.

take something along

Bring something with you.

ExampleI’ll take the draft along to the meeting in case we need it.
NoteNatural, but for people, bring someone along is often more idiomatic.

take someone along

Include someone or help them follow the thinking.

ExampleIf we change direction, we need to take the rest of the team along with us.
RhetoricUseful when talking about buy-in and persuasion.
Chapter 03

Go, come, bring: movement words that carry arguments

go along with

Agree to or accept a proposal.

ExampleI can go along with that, as long as we review it in two weeks.
LogicConditional agreement.

go with

Choose an option.

ExampleI’d go with the simpler version.
ToneVery natural; often better than I would select.

go through

Review, experience, or examine.

ExampleLet’s go through the assumptions one by one.

go over

Review together.

ExampleCan we go over the last two slides again?

go back to

Return to an earlier point.

ExampleCan we go back to what you said about cost?

go too far

Overstate or overreact.

ExampleI agree it’s a problem, but I think calling it a failure goes too far.
RhetoricChallenges degree, not the whole claim.

come up with

Create an idea, answer, or solution.

ExampleCan we come up with something simpler?

come down to

Ultimately depend on one key issue.

ExampleFor me, it comes down to timing.
LogicCompresses a complex argument into the decisive factor.

come across as

Create an impression.

ExampleThe message is clear, but it might come across as a bit defensive.
PragmaticsGreat for discussing tone without accusing someone of bad intent.

come back to

Return to a point later or repeatedly.

ExampleI keep coming back to the same question: who’s actually going to use this?

bring up

Introduce a topic.

ExampleI wanted to bring up one thing before we finish.

bring someone in

Include another person in a discussion.

ExampleLet’s bring Maya in before we make the final call.

bring something forward

Move something earlier or present it for consideration.

ExampleCould we bring the review forward by a week?

bring it back to

Refocus the conversation.

ExampleJust to bring it back to the original question, do we actually need this now?
Chapter 04

Work, sort, figure, iron: practical problem-solving language

work out

Find a solution or reach an arrangement.

ExampleWe can probably work something out.
ToneCollaborative and low-friction.

work around

Find a way to operate despite a problem.

ExampleIt’s not ideal, but we can work around it for now.

work through

Deal with a problem step by step.

ExampleLet’s work through the edge cases before we launch.

figure out

Understand or solve.

ExampleWe need to figure out why people are dropping off here.
ToneProfessional-safe in almost any spoken setting.

sort out

Resolve or organize.

ExampleI’ll sort out the access issue this afternoon.
Regional noteEspecially common in British and international workplace English.

iron out

Resolve small remaining problems.

ExampleThe main idea works. We just need to iron out a few details.

clear up

Remove confusion.

ExampleCan we clear up one thing before we move on?

nail down

Make something precise or final.

ExampleWe haven’t nailed down the scope yet.
ToneCommon and professional, though slightly more informal.

pin down

Identify precisely.

ExampleIt’s hard to pin down the exact cause.

tighten up

Make something more precise or controlled.

ExampleThe argument’s good. I’d just tighten up the middle section.
Chapter 05

Set, lay, line, map: organizing thoughts without sounding formal

set out

Explain or present clearly.

ExampleCan you set out the two options in plain English?

set up

Arrange, establish, or prepare.

ExampleI’ll set up a quick call with Legal.

lay out

Present the structure or reasoning.

ExampleLet me lay out why I’m hesitant.
RhetoricSignals a short argument without sounding academic.

spell out

Make explicit.

ExampleCan we spell out what success actually means here?
LogicForces vague claims into testable terms.

line up

Arrange people, resources, or facts.

ExampleWe need to line up the owners before we announce anything.

map out

Plan the steps.

ExampleLet’s map out what happens after launch.

break down

Divide into smaller parts.

ExampleCan we break the problem down a bit?

boil down to

Reduce to the essential point.

ExampleIt really boils down to one question: is the extra cost worth it?

sum up

Give the short version.

ExampleSo, just to sum up, we’re keeping the scope but moving the date.
Chapter 06

Checking, following, circling: keeping work moving

check in

Make brief contact or ask for an update.

ExampleI’ll check in with Sam tomorrow.

follow up

Continue after an earlier interaction.

ExampleI’ll follow up once I’ve got the numbers.

follow through

Actually complete what was promised.

ExampleThe idea was fine. The problem was we didn’t follow through.

get back to

Reply later with an answer.

ExampleI’m not sure off the top of my head. Can I get back to you?

circle back

Return to a topic later.

ExampleLet’s circle back once we’ve heard from the client.
CautionNormal, but overused corporate jargon. Use return to or come back to if you want plainer speech.

touch base

Make brief contact.

ExampleCan we touch base after lunch?
CautionWidely understood, but can sound corporate. Check in is usually fresher.

keep someone posted

Give them updates.

ExampleI’ll keep you posted if anything changes.

keep an eye on

Watch something over time.

ExampleLet’s keep an eye on conversion for the next few days.
Chapter 07

Soft disagreement: strong logic, low social friction

Yeah, but…

Acknowledge, then object.

ExampleYeah, but does that actually solve the problem?

That’s fair, but…

Concede a valid point before limiting it.

ExampleThat’s fair, but I don’t think it gets us all the way there.

I’m not sure I’d go that far.

Challenge an overstatement.

ExampleI agree adoption is slow. I’m not sure I’d go that far and call the launch a failure.

Not necessarily.

Reject an inference rather than a fact.

ExampleSales are down. But that doesn’t necessarily mean demand is down.

I don’t know if that follows.

Question the logical link.

ExampleThey missed one deadline. I don’t know if it follows that the whole process is broken.

Are we sure that’s the problem?

Challenge the diagnosis.

ExampleAre we sure that’s the problem? It could just be poor onboarding.

I see the logic. I’m just not convinced by the conclusion.

Separate reasoning structure from final judgment.

ExampleI see the logic. I’m just not convinced the cost saving is big enough to justify the change.

I’d separate those two things.

Prevent two claims from being treated as one.

ExampleI’d separate speed from quality. We can improve one without sacrificing the other.

That might be true, but it’s a different point.

Reject a distraction without denying it.

ExampleThat might be true, but it’s a different point. We’re talking about whether this deadline is realistic.

I’m with you up to a point.

Partial agreement.

ExampleI’m with you up to a point. I just don’t think every team needs the same rule.
Chapter 08

Questioning without sounding like an interrogation

What makes you say that?

Ask for the basis of a claim.

ExampleYou think customers won’t pay for it? What makes you say that?

How do you mean?

Ask someone to clarify their meaning.

ExampleYou said the process feels heavy. How do you mean?

What are you basing that on?

Ask for evidence.

ExampleYou think we’ll lose half the users. What are you basing that on?

How does that get us there?

Test whether a proposal reaches the goal.

ExampleOkay, but how does adding another approval step get us there?

What would have to be true for that to work?

Expose hidden assumptions.

ExampleWhat would have to be true for that plan to work?

What are we assuming here?

Surface assumptions.

ExampleBefore we decide, what are we assuming here about demand?

Say we do that. Then what?

Test consequences.

ExampleSay we cut the price. Then what? Do we actually know volume will make up for it?

What’s the downside?

Ask for costs or risks.

ExampleI can see the upside. What’s the downside?

What’s stopping us from…?

Probe a simpler alternative.

ExampleWhat’s stopping us from testing this with one team first?

Can you walk me through that?

Ask for reasoning step by step.

ExampleI’m not quite following the jump there. Can you walk me through it?

Where are you going with this?

Ask for the intended conclusion.

ExampleI see the numbers, but where are you going with this?
CautionCan sound impatient. Softer: What do you think this means for us?

What would change your mind?

Test whether a position is evidence-sensitive.

ExampleWhat would change your mind on this?
Chapter 09

Calibrating certainty: sound precise, not weak

I think…

Ordinary claim marker.

ExampleI think we’re solving the wrong problem.

I’d say…

Personal judgment, slightly lighter.

ExampleI’d say the second option is safer.

It looks like…

Evidence suggests, but does not prove.

ExampleIt looks like most of the drop happened on mobile.

From what I can tell…

Judgment based on currently available information.

ExampleFrom what I can tell, the delay is mostly on our side.

As far as I can see…

Similar to from what I can tell.

ExampleAs far as I can see, there’s no technical blocker.

probably

More likely than not.

ExampleWe’ll probably need another round of testing.

maybe

Possible, tentative.

ExampleMaybe we’re asking too much in the first step.

pretty much

Almost entirely.

ExampleThat pretty much covers the main risks.

more or less

Approximately or broadly.

ExampleThe scope is more or less fixed now.

at least for now

Limit a claim in time.

ExampleI’d keep the current process, at least for now.
Chapter 10

Tiny connectors that make spoken reasoning flow

the thing is

Introduce the real complication.

ExampleI’d be open to it. The thing is, we don’t have anyone to own it.

I mean

Clarify, soften, or reformulate.

ExampleI mean, the idea’s fine. I just don’t think now is the right time.

still

Concede something but maintain your position.

ExampleIt’s cheaper, sure. Still, I’d rather pay a bit more for something reliable.

though

Add a light contrast, often at the end.

ExampleI like the direction. The timeline feels tight, though.

either way

Show that the conclusion survives two possibilities.

ExampleMaybe it’s a pricing issue, maybe it isn’t. Either way, we need better data.

at the same time

Hold two truths together.

ExampleI get why we want speed. At the same time, we can’t skip basic testing.

then again

Introduce a counter-consideration.

ExampleWe could wait. Then again, waiting has a cost too.

if anything

Suggest the opposite may be closer to true.

ExampleI don’t think the change made things simpler. If anything, it added another step.

for one thing

Introduce one reason without implying it’s the only reason.

ExampleI wouldn’t launch yet. For one thing, support isn’t ready.

that said

Concede before shifting position.

ExampleThe first test was weak. That said, the second one looked much better.
NoteA little more polished than everyday speech, but still common professionally.
Chapter 11

Useful colloquial chunks for meetings

Where are we on…?

Ask for status.

ExampleWhere are we on the vendor review?

What are we looking at?

Ask about size, timing, or scope.

ExampleBest case, what are we looking at — two weeks?

What do we need from them?

Clarify dependency.

ExampleBefore we email them, what do we actually need from them?

Who’s got this?

Ask who owns a task.

ExampleOkay, who’s got the follow-up with Legal?
ToneFine in familiar teams. Softer with senior groups: Who’s taking the lead on this?

Are we good with…?

Check agreement.

ExampleAre we good with Friday, or do we need more time?

I’m good with that.

Signal agreement.

ExampleYeah, I’m good with that. Let’s keep it simple.

That works for me.

Accept a plan.

ExampleThree o’clock? That works for me.

I can live with that.

Accept something that is not ideal.

ExampleIt’s not perfect, but I can live with that.
PragmaticsShows compromise, not enthusiasm.

Let’s leave it there.

Close a topic.

ExampleI think we’ve got enough for today. Let’s leave it there and pick it up tomorrow.

Let’s park that.

Temporarily postpone a topic.

ExampleUseful point. Let’s park that for now and finish the pricing discussion.
CautionCommon, but slightly corporate.

pick it up later

Resume later.

ExampleWe’re running out of time. Let’s pick it up tomorrow.

Chapter 13

Rhetorical patterns you can reuse

Agree → limit → position

Useful when you want to disagree without sounding combative.

PatternYeah, that’s fair. I just don’t think it’s the whole story.
ExampleYeah, cost matters. I just don’t think cost is the main problem.

Claim → reason → short conclusion

A clean spoken argument.

PatternI’d wait. We’re still finding bugs. Give it a few days.

Question → implication

Make the other person inspect the logic.

PatternAre we sure users want this? Because if they don’t, the rest doesn’t really matter.

Concede → pivot

Accept a fact without giving up your conclusion.

PatternSure, it’s faster. But if it creates more support work, I’m not sure we gain much.

Alternative explanation

Block a premature conclusion.

PatternMaybe. Or it could just be that the sample’s too small.

Degree challenge

Disagree with intensity, not direction.

PatternI agree it’s a problem. I just wouldn’t call it a crisis.

Assumption test

Expose what has to be true.

PatternThat works if demand stays flat. Do we know that?

Consequence test

Follow an idea one step further.

PatternOkay, say we do that. What happens when volume doubles?

Definition test

Make vague words concrete.

PatternWhen you say urgent, do you mean today or this week?

Comparison test

Force a fair baseline.

PatternBetter than what? The current process, or the original plan?
Chapter 14

Mini-dialogues: real rhythm in professional speech

Challenging a rushed decision

AI think we should launch Friday.
BMaybe. I’m just not sure we know enough yet.
AWhat are we missing?
BSupport volume, mainly. We’ve only tested with a small group. I’d give it another week.

Pushing back on a deadline

ACan you get this done by tomorrow?
BI can get you a rough version. A finished one by tomorrow’s probably a stretch.
AWhat would you need?
BOne more day. Then I can actually check the numbers instead of guessing.

Questioning an assumption

APeople aren’t using it because the UI is confusing.
BCould be. What are we basing that on?
AMostly support tickets.
BRight. I’d still check the usage data before we redesign anything.

Disagreeing with a senior colleague

AWe should standardize this across every team.
BI can see why. I’m just not sure every team has the same problem.
ASo what would you do?
BStart with the two teams that are struggling. If it works there, then roll it out.

Recovering when you misspoke

ASo you think the idea won’t work?
BNo — not exactly. I mean, it could work. I just think we’re trying it too early.

Closing a messy discussion

AWe’ve got three options and no clear winner.
BYeah. So maybe don’t force it today. Let’s narrow it down to two, get the numbers, and pick it up tomorrow.
Chapter 15

What to avoid or use carefully

super casual slang

Can sound careless or socially mismatched.

AvoidNah, whatever, dude, kinda sucks, you guys messed this up.
BetterI’m not sure that works. / That caused a few problems. / I think we need to revisit it.

corporate clichés

Not wrong, but they can make natural speech feel canned.

Overusedcircle back, touch base, move the needle, low-hanging fruit, take this offline, synergy
Plainercome back to it, check in, make a real difference, easiest win, talk after the meeting

empty softeners

Too many make you sound unsure.

WeakI was just kind of wondering if maybe we could possibly…
BetterCould we try a smaller test first?

fake certainty

Professional speech often sounds stronger when it is accurately calibrated.

Too strongThis will definitely fail.
BetterI think this is pretty likely to fail unless we fix the onboarding.

essay transitions

They are grammatical, but often unnatural in live conversation.

Writtentherefore, furthermore, consequently, nevertheless
Spokenso, and, because, but, still, the thing is
Chapter 16

Fast drills for mastering the chunks

Drill 1 — formal to spoken

Turn the formal sentence into something a colleague would actually say.

FormalI would recommend postponing implementation until additional evidence is available.
SpokenI’d hold off until we’ve got more data.

Drill 2 — add logic

Start with a natural reaction, then give one reason.

PromptYou disagree with a plan because the sample is too small.
AnswerYeah, I’m not convinced yet. We’ve only tested it with ten people.

Drill 3 — challenge the link

Question the inference, not the person.

PromptOne customer complained, so your colleague says the whole product is confusing.
AnswerMaybe, but does one complaint really tell us that?

Drill 4 — test an assumption

Use what would have to be true or are we assuming.

AnswerThat works if demand stays steady. Are we sure it will?

Drill 5 — close cleanly

End with a short practical conclusion.

AnswerSo… I’d wait. / Let’s test it first. / I’d keep it simple. / Give it a week.
Built for spoken practice. Read the examples aloud. Keep the rhythm short: reaction → point → reason → close.
