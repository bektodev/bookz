Real Spoken English

A practical guide to everyday conversation, workplace talk, arguments, coffee chats, awkward moments, and difficult conversations

The goal of this guide is not to make you sound educated.

It is not to make you sound formal.

It is not to make you sound like you memorized business English from a textbook.

The goal is much simpler:

Think clearly. Speak naturally. Respond quickly. Make your point without sounding robotic.

Good spoken English often sounds like this:

  • Yeah, I get that.
  • I mean, maybe.
  • The thing is...
  • I don't know about that.
  • That's fair.
  • But here's the problem.
  • What if we just...
  • I wouldn't go that far.
  • I see what you mean.
  • I'm not sure that's gonna work.
  • Anyway, we'll see.

The sentences are simple.

The thinking behind them doesn't have to be simple.

Part 1 — What Spoken English Actually Is

1. Spoken English is built in pieces

Written English:

I believe the current approach should be reconsidered because it creates unnecessary operational complexity.

Spoken English:

Yeah, I'm not sure this is the best way to do it. It just feels like we're making things harder than they need to be.

Same idea.

Different delivery.

Real speech usually comes in chunks:

  • reaction
  • thought
  • reason
  • example
  • correction
  • conclusion

For example:

Yeah, maybe. I mean, I get why we're doing it. I just don't think we need to do it right now.

That is not badly organized English.

It is natural spoken reasoning.

Part 2 — The Logic Underneath Conversation

Logic

Definition: How your reasons support your conclusion.

You do not need to say:

Premise one... Premise two... Therefore...

You think that way internally.

Then you speak naturally.

Internal logic

  • We don't have enough people.
  • The new project requires more work.
  • The current team is already busy.
  • Therefore, starting now may cause problems.

Spoken version

I don't know. We're already pretty stretched, and this is gonna add even more work. I'd probably wait until we have a bit more capacity.

The logic is still there.

It is simply hidden inside conversation.

Part 3 — Your Basic Conversational Logic Patterns

These patterns are your foundation.

Pattern 1 — Reaction → Reason

Use it when somebody asks for your opinion.

Yeah, probably. I just think it'll be easier that way.

Not really. It seems like a lot of work for very little benefit.

Maybe. I mean, it depends on how much it costs.

Logic

  • conclusion
  • reason

Simple.

Very common.

Pattern 2 — Reaction → Reason → Example

I don't think we need another meeting. We're already talking about this almost every day. Like, yesterday we spent an hour going over basically the same stuff.

Logic

  • claim: no extra meeting
  • reason: communication already happens frequently
  • evidence: yesterday's meeting

Pattern 3 — Yeah, but...

Probably one of the most important structures in conversational English.

Yeah, but that's assuming everything goes according to plan.

Yeah, but do we actually know that?

Yeah, but that's not really the issue.

Yeah, but then we're back to the same problem.

Logic

Usually:

  • acknowledge something
  • challenge an assumption
  • introduce another factor

Pattern 4 — That's true, but...

Slightly softer than yeah, but.

That's true, but we still have to think about the cost.

That's true, but I'm not sure it changes the overall problem.

Excellent for professional discussions.

Pattern 5 — Maybe, but...

Useful when you're uncertain or don't want to sound aggressive.

Maybe, but I wouldn't assume everyone's gonna use it.

Maybe, but that feels a little risky.

Maybe, but couldn't we try something smaller first?

Part 4 — Coffee Talk

Coffee talk is important because there is often no clear topic and no clear goal.

You are simply keeping the conversation moving.

That requires a different skill.

1. Don't answer too completely

Someone says:

How was your weekend?

Bad conversational answer:

It was good.

The conversation dies.

Better:

Pretty good. Didn't do much, honestly. Just stayed home, watched some stuff. What about you?

Now there are multiple openings:

  • staying home
  • movies
  • weekend
  • their weekend

2. Give people something to pick up

Instead of

Yeah, I like traveling.

Try:

Yeah, I love it. I just hate the airport part.

Now the other person can talk about airports.

Instead of

I had a busy weekend.

Try:

Yeah, it was pretty busy. I had people over Saturday, and then Sunday I basically did nothing.

Now they have material.

Part 5 — Easy Coffee-Talk Structures

Observation → Comment → Question

It's actually really quiet today. Usually this place is packed. Do you come here often?

Answer → Small detail → Return question

Yeah, I've been here a few times. Their coffee's pretty good. What about you?

Agreement → Personal detail

Yeah, exactly. Winters here are rough. I don't mind the cold, but the darkness gets me.

Small disagreement → Humor

Really? You like waking up early? I don't understand you people.

Friendly disagreement can create conversation.

Part 6 — When You Don't Know What to Say

You do not always need a brilliant response.

Use reaction language.

  • Really?
  • No way.
  • That's crazy.
  • That's rough.
  • Nice.
  • That's actually pretty cool.
  • Makes sense.
  • Fair enough.
  • Yeah, I can see that.
  • Huh, interesting.
  • I didn't know that.
  • That's funny.
  • That's annoying.
  • That's a good point.

Then add one small question.

Really? How'd that happen?

That's rough. What did you end up doing?

Interesting. Do you actually like it?

Part 7 — Asking Better Questions

Questions keep conversation alive.

But too many questions can make you sound like you're conducting an interview.

Instead of:

Where are you from?

How long have you lived here?

What do you do?

Do you like it?

Mix questions with comments.

Better

A: Where are you from?

B: Spain.

A: Oh nice. I've only been to Barcelona, but I loved it. What part are you from?

Now you contributed something too.

Part 8 — Follow-Up Questions

Useful spoken forms:

  • How come?
  • Really? Why?
  • What happened?
  • And then what?
  • How was that?
  • Was it worth it?
  • Did you like it?
  • How'd you get into that?
  • What made you do that?
  • So what happened in the end?
  • Was that always the plan?

These sound much more conversational than constantly asking long, perfectly formed questions.

Part 9 — Thinking While Speaking

You need language that buys you thinking time.

Useful thinking markers

  • I mean...
  • I guess...
  • I don't know...
  • Let me think.
  • Probably...
  • Maybe...
  • Honestly...
  • Actually...
  • The thing is...
  • I suppose...
  • It's hard to say.
  • That's a good question.
  • I haven't really thought about it.

Example:

Would you ever move to another country?

Hmm. I don't know. I mean, maybe for a couple years. I don't think I'd want to move permanently, though.

You are forming the answer while speaking.

Natural.

Part 10 — Correcting Yourself

Native speakers constantly change direction mid-sentence.

Use:

  • Actually...
  • Well, no...
  • Or rather...
  • I mean...
  • Maybe that's not the right word.
  • Let me put it another way.
  • What I mean is...

Example

It was really difficult. Well, not difficult exactly. It was just really time-consuming.

Very natural.

You do not need to produce the perfect sentence immediately.

Part 11 — Disagreeing

Disagreement is where logic and conversational English really meet.

Level 1 — Very soft

Hmm, I'm not sure.

Maybe.

I don't know about that.

I'm not totally convinced.

Level 2 — Normal disagreement

I don't think that's the issue.

I see it a little differently.

I wouldn't go that far.

I don't think that's gonna work.

Level 3 — Stronger

No, I really don't agree with that.

I think that's the wrong way to look at it.

I don't think that's fair.

That's not what happened.

Choose based on the situation.

Part 12 — Disagreeing Without Creating a Fight

Use:

Acknowledge → disagreement → reason

Example

Yeah, I get what you're saying. I just don't think more meetings are gonna fix the problem. People already have too many meetings.

Logic:

  • recognize their concern
  • reject solution
  • explain why

Part 13 — Agreeing With the Problem, Not the Solution

Very useful.

Yeah, I agree there's a problem. I'm just not sure that's the way to fix it.

Example:

A: We need everybody back in the office.

B: I agree communication hasn't been great. I'm just not sure bringing everyone in five days a week is the answer.

Excellent professional conversational structure.

Part 14 — Challenging Assumptions

This is one of the strongest reasoning skills.

Someone says:

If we lower the price, we'll get more customers.

Instead of immediately disagreeing:

Maybe, but how do we know price is the problem?

Or:

Are we sure that's why people aren't buying?

You are attacking the assumption, not the person.

Useful forms:

  • Are we sure about that?
  • Do we actually know that?
  • What are we basing that on?
  • But is that really the reason?
  • That's assuming X, right?
  • What if X doesn't happen?
  • How likely is that, though?

Part 15 — Challenging the Conclusion

Sometimes you accept the evidence but not the conclusion.

Okay, but does that mean we need to change the whole system?

Example:

A: Three customers complained about checkout.

B: Sure, but does that mean we need a complete redesign? Maybe we should first figure out exactly what's causing the problem.

Logic:

complaints exist ≠ complete redesign is automatically necessary

Part 16 — Asking for Evidence

You don't need to sound like a courtroom lawyer.

Say:

  • How do we know that?
  • Where's that coming from?
  • What are we basing that on?
  • Do we have any numbers on that?
  • Have customers actually said that?
  • Is that something we've tested?

Part 17 — Coffee Talk With Someone You Don't Like

This happens.

You don't need to become their friend.

Your goal is simply to keep things comfortable.

Safe topics

  • weekend
  • food
  • weather
  • travel
  • commute
  • local events
  • entertainment
  • holidays
  • work topics that aren't sensitive

Example

A: Morning.

B: Morning. How's it going?

A: Not bad.

B: Same. Feels like everyone's half asleep today.

A: Yeah, seriously.

That's enough.

Not every interaction needs depth.

Part 18 — Escaping a Conversation Politely

Useful phrases:

  • Anyway, I should probably get back to it.
  • Alright, I'll let you get back to work.
  • I should grab some food.
  • I've gotta jump into something.
  • Anyway, good talking to you.
  • I'll catch you later.
  • Alright, see you around.

Example:

Anyway, I should probably get back upstairs. Good talking to you.

Very normal.

Part 19 — When Someone Talks Too Much

You sometimes need to interrupt.

Soft interruptions:

  • Sorry, just one thing.
  • Can I jump in for a second?
  • Hang on, though.
  • Just to clarify...
  • Before we move on...
  • Can I stop you there for a second?

Example:

Sorry, can I jump in for a second? I think we're mixing up two different issues.

Part 20 — When You Are Interrupted

Don't panic.

Say:

  • Yeah, go ahead.
  • Sure.
  • Sorry, you go.
  • No, finish your point.
  • Yeah, what were you gonna say?

Then continue later:

Yeah, so what I was saying was...

Anyway, my point was...

Part 21 — Getting Back to Your Point

Very useful:

  • Anyway...
  • So...
  • Like I was saying...
  • Going back to...
  • The point is...
  • What I'm getting at is...
  • Basically...
  • So yeah...

Example:

Anyway, the point is, I don't think we need to decide today.

Part 22 — Clarifying Yourself

What I mean is...

I'm not saying we shouldn't do it. What I mean is, I don't think we should do it right now.

This distinction is extremely useful.

I'm not saying X. I'm saying Y.

I'm not saying the idea is bad. I'm saying we haven't tested it enough.

This is conversational logic.

You are separating two claims.

Part 23 — The Powerful It's Not X, It's Y Structure

Examples:

It's not really a money problem. It's more of a timing problem.

It's not that I don't trust him. I just want to check the numbers myself.

It's not about working harder. It's about working on the right things.

Excellent for reframing.

Part 24 — Difficult Conversations

Now we get into conversations you may not enjoy but cannot avoid.

Examples:

  • telling someone their work isn't good enough
  • pushing back on a manager
  • asking someone to stop doing something
  • talking about money
  • telling someone you're unhappy
  • addressing rude behavior
  • saying no
  • discussing missed deadlines
  • dealing with blame
  • admitting a mistake

The language can stay simple.

Part 25 — Saying No

Don't over-explain.

Casual

Yeah, I don't think I can. I've got too much going on this week.

Work

I don't think I can take that on right now. I'm already pretty full with the other project.

Stronger boundary

I can't commit to that deadline. It's just not realistic with what we have right now.

Part 26 — Saying No While Offering Something Else

I can't do Friday, but I could probably get it to you Monday.

I can't take the whole thing on, but I can help with the first part.

Logic:

reject request + offer realistic alternative

Part 27 — Giving Negative Feedback

Avoid long formal introductions.

Try:

Hey, one thing I noticed...

I think this part needs another pass.

I'm having a little trouble following this section.

I think we need to tighten this up before we send it.

Example

I think the main idea is good. I'm just having trouble following the middle part. It kind of jumps from one point to another.

Natural and specific.

Part 28 — Receiving Criticism

Useful responses:

  • Yeah, that's fair.
  • I can see that.
  • Okay, I get what you mean.
  • That's helpful.
  • Yeah, I probably could've handled that better.
  • I hadn't thought about it that way.
  • Let me take another look.

You do not need to defend yourself immediately.

Part 29 — When You Disagree With Criticism

I get the concern. I'm just not sure I agree with that part.

Then explain.

From my side, the reason I did it that way was...

Example:

I get why it looked rushed. From my side, though, we had two days to get it out, so I focused on the parts that were actually blocking the launch.

Part 30 — Admitting a Mistake

Keep it simple.

Yeah, that was on me. I missed it.

You're right. I should've caught that.

Yeah, I messed that up. I'll fix it.

No giant speech required.

Part 31 — Explaining Without Making Excuses

Useful structure:

Own it → context → solution

Yeah, I missed the deadline. That's on me. I underestimated how long the testing would take. I'll have the updated version tomorrow morning.

Good logic.

No unnecessary drama.

Part 32 — When Someone Blames You

Don't immediately attack them.

First separate facts.

Hang on. I don't think that's exactly what happened.

I think we're mixing up two things here.

That's not quite how I remember it.

Then explain:

I sent the final numbers Tuesday. The delay happened after that.

Part 33 — When Someone Is Being Unfair

Useful language:

  • I don't think that's fair.
  • I don't think that's a fair way to put it.
  • That's not really what happened.
  • I think that's missing some context.
  • I don't think that's all on me.
  • We need to separate what I was responsible for from what happened afterward.

Notice the language is not complicated.

The reasoning does the work.

Part 34 — When Someone Is Rude

You can respond without becoming rude yourself.

Mild

Okay, there's no need for that.

Direct

I don't think that comment was necessary.

Boundary

I'm happy to talk about the problem, but not like this.

Strong

If we're gonna have this conversation, let's keep it respectful.

Part 35 — Asking Someone to Stop Doing Something

Structure:

Behavior → effect → request

Hey, can you give me a heads-up before changing those files? I was working on the same section yesterday and it caused a bunch of conflicts.

Simple.

Clear.

Part 36 — Talking to Your Manager

You usually want to sound clear, not submissive.

Raising a concern

I just wanna flag something. I'm not sure we're gonna hit Friday with the current scope.

Pushback

I can do Friday if we cut a couple things. If everything stays in, I think Monday is more realistic.

Asking for priorities

I've got three things that all seem urgent. Which one do you want me to prioritize?

Excellent workplace English.

Part 37 — Disagreeing With Your Manager

You don't need:

With all due respect...

Often that sounds more confrontational than intended.

Try:

I see what you're saying. My only concern is...

Or:

Yeah, I can do that. I'm just worried about what happens to X.

Or:

I'm not sure that's gonna get us the result we want.

Part 38 — Being Challenged in a Meeting

Someone says:

Why didn't you catch this earlier?

Instead of panicking:

Fair question. At the time, the numbers looked fine. The issue only showed up once we got the latest data.

Structure:

acknowledge → context → explanation

Part 39 — When You Don't Know the Answer

Say it.

I don't know. I'd have to check.

I'm not sure off the top of my head.

I don't wanna guess. Let me check the numbers.

I haven't looked at that yet.

These sound much better than pretending.

Part 40 — When You Only Partly Know

I don't know the exact number, but it's somewhere around 20%.

I could be wrong, but I think it was sometime in May.

From what I remember, yes, but I'd have to double-check.

This is epistemic accuracy.

Epistemic accuracy

Definition: Matching your confidence to what you actually know.

Strong communicators don't pretend every thought is a fact.

Part 41 — Levels of Certainty

Strong

  • definitely
  • clearly
  • I'm sure
  • there's no doubt

Medium

  • probably
  • I think
  • most likely
  • it looks like

Weak

  • maybe
  • possibly
  • I guess
  • I'm not sure, but...

Example:

Don't say:

Customers definitely hate the new design.

If you only have three complaints.

Say:

We've had a few complaints, so there might be an issue with the new design.

Better logic.

Part 42 — Avoiding Overgeneralization

Bad:

People hate meetings.

Better:

A lot of people seem frustrated with the number of meetings we have.

Bad:

Nobody understands the product.

Better:

Some customers seem confused about what the product actually does.

Good speakers are careful with:

  • everyone
  • nobody
  • always
  • never
  • obviously
  • definitely

Part 43 — Making a Case

You don't need a speech.

Use:

Here's the problem...

The reason I'm worried is...

If we do X, then...

So I'd rather...

Example:

Here's the problem. If we launch Friday, we have basically no time to test the final build. So if something breaks, we're dealing with it over the weekend. I'd rather push it to Monday.

That is a complete argument.

Part 44 — Cause and Effect

Core forms:

If X, then Y.

Because X, Y happens.

The more X, the more Y.

X is gonna lead to Y.

That's why Y happens.

Examples:

If we keep adding features, we're never gonna ship.

The more meetings we add, the less time people have to actually work.

That'll probably create more problems later.

Part 45 — Trade-Offs

Trade-off

Definition: Getting one benefit means accepting another cost.

Useful language:

  • The downside is...
  • The trade-off is...
  • The problem with that is...
  • We'd gain X, but we'd lose Y.
  • It's faster, but it's also riskier.

Example:

Yeah, we could ship Friday. The trade-off is we'd have almost no testing time.

Part 46 — Comparing Options

Don't overcomplicate it.

Option A is cheaper, but it'll take longer. Option B costs more, but we'd probably have it done this month. I'd go with B.

Logic:

comparison → relevant difference → choice

Part 47 — Priorities

Useful language:

  • The main thing is...
  • The bigger issue is...
  • What matters most is...
  • That's not really the priority right now.
  • I'd focus on X first.
  • We can deal with Y later.

Example:

The design isn't perfect, but honestly, that's not the main issue right now. We need to make sure the thing actually works.

Part 48 — Reframing

Reframing

Definition: Changing the question or perspective.

Example:

I don't think the question is whether we can do it. We probably can. The question is whether it's worth doing.

Another:

This isn't really about who's right. We just need to figure out what went wrong.

Powerful rhetoric.

Simple English.

Part 49 — Concessions

Concession

Definition: Accepting part of the other person's argument.

Useful forms:

  • Yeah, that's true.
  • That's fair.
  • I agree with that part.
  • Sure.
  • I can see that.
  • You have a point there.

Then:

but...

Example:

Yeah, that's true. It would save money. But it might also slow us down.

Concessions make your arguments feel less rigid.

Part 50 — Partial Agreement

I agree up to a point.

In more casual speech:

Yeah, kind of.

I agree with some of that.

I think you're right about X. I'm just not sure about Y.

Example:

I think you're right that we need better communication. I'm just not convinced another daily meeting is the answer.

Part 51 — False Choices

Someone may present only two options:

Either we launch now or we lose the customer.

Challenge it:

Are those really the only two options?

Couldn't we give them a smaller version first?

Logic:

A or B may not be the full option set.

Part 52 — Slippery Predictions

Someone says:

If we let people work from home one more day, nobody's gonna come into the office anymore.

Response:

I don't know if it necessarily goes that far.

Or:

That's a pretty big jump.

Excellent conversational logic phrase:

That's a pretty big jump.

Meaning:

the conclusion goes much further than the evidence.

Part 53 — Correlation vs Cause

Correlation

Two things happen together.

Cause

One thing makes the other happen.

Conversation:

Sales went up after we changed the website.

Response:

Yeah, but do we know the website caused it? We also launched that promotion at the same time.

Good reasoning.

Natural language.

Part 54 — Anecdotes vs Evidence

Someone:

My friend tried it and hated it.

Response:

Sure, but that's one person. Do we know what customers overall think?

You don't need to say:

That evidence is anecdotal.

You can simply say:

That's one example, though.

Part 55 — Moving the Goalposts

Someone changes the standard after you meet the original one.

Example:

A: We need 100 responses before we decide.

Later:

A: Yeah, but 100 isn't enough.

Response:

Okay, but we agreed 100 was enough before we started. If we're changing the standard now, we should be clear about why.

Part 56 — Avoiding Personal Attacks

Bad:

You always make things complicated.

Better:

I think this process is getting more complicated than it needs to be.

Attack the idea or behavior.

Not the person.

Part 57 — When Conversation Gets Emotional

Slow the argument down.

Useful phrases:

  • Okay, I hear you.
  • I get why you're frustrated.
  • I'm not trying to argue with you.
  • I'm trying to understand what happened.
  • Can we separate these two things?
  • I think we're talking past each other.
  • I don't think we're disagreeing about everything here.

Part 58 — We're Talking Past Each Other

Excellent phrase.

Definition: Two people are arguing about different interpretations of the issue.

Example:

I think we're talking past each other. You're talking about whether the idea is good. I'm talking about whether we can actually do it this month.

Very useful.

Part 59 — De-Escalating

Sometimes winning the argument is not the goal.

Useful language:

Okay, I think we agree on more than we disagree on.

We're probably not gonna settle this right now.

Let's come back to it after we've looked at the numbers.

I don't want this to turn into a bigger thing than it needs to be.

Part 60 — Ending an Unproductive Argument

I think we're going in circles.

I don't think we're gonna solve this right now.

Let's leave it there for now.

We probably need more information before we keep arguing about it.

Part 61 — Conversations About Money

Money conversations often feel awkward.

Keep them factual.

Salary

I wanted to talk about compensation. My role's changed quite a bit over the last year, and I've taken on a lot more responsibility. I'd like to talk about whether my salary still matches the role.

More conversational:

I wanted to ask about salary. I've taken on quite a bit more over the last year, so I wanted to see if there's room to adjust it.

Part 62 — Asking Someone to Pay You Back

Hey, just a reminder about the €40 from dinner the other night. Can you send it when you get a chance?

If necessary:

Hey, can you send that €40 today? I don't wanna forget about it.

No giant explanation.

Part 63 — Saying Something Is Too Expensive

That's a bit more than I wanted to spend.

Yeah, that's probably outside my budget.

I like it, but I don't think I can justify the price.

Part 64 — Social Invitations You Don't Want

Thanks, but I think I'm gonna pass this time.

I don't think I can make it, but thanks for inviting me.

I'm probably gonna have a quiet weekend, honestly.

No fake story needed.

Part 65 — Someone Pushes After You Say No

Come on, just come for an hour.

Response:

Yeah, I know. I just really don't feel like going out tonight. Maybe next time.

If they continue:

No, seriously, I'm gonna sit this one out.

Part 66 — Awkward Silence

You don't need to fill every silence.

But if you want to:

  • So, what are you up to this weekend?
  • Anyway, how's work been?
  • Have you been watching anything good lately?
  • How's everything going with X?
  • Oh, by the way...

Part 67 — Changing the Subject

Useful phrases:

  • Anyway...
  • Speaking of that...
  • By the way...
  • Oh, before I forget...
  • That reminds me...
  • On a completely different note...

Example:

Oh, speaking of travel, did you ever book that trip to Italy?

Part 68 — When You Forget a Word

Do not freeze.

Use description.

What's the word... the thing you use to...

I can't remember what it's called.

You know, the thing that...

What's his name again?

Example:

I need one of those... what do you call it... the thing that lets you plug three devices into one socket.

This is real communication.

Part 69 — When You Don't Understand Someone

Don't pretend.

  • Sorry?
  • What was that?
  • Sorry, I missed that.
  • Could you say that again?
  • What do you mean?
  • Sorry, what does X mean?
  • I didn't catch the last part.
  • So you mean...?

Example:

Sorry, when you say it's delayed, do you mean by a day or by a couple weeks?

Excellent clarification.

Part 70 — Checking Your Understanding

Use:

So basically...

So you're saying...

If I understand you right...

Just so I'm clear...

Example:

So basically, we can launch Friday, but only if we drop those two features. Right?

Very useful in work.

Part 71 — Summarizing a Messy Discussion

Someone needs to organize the chaos.

Okay, so it sounds like we agree on two things. We need to fix the payment issue, and we don't want to delay the whole launch. The only question is how much we can realistically fix before Friday.

This is conversational leadership.

Part 72 — Storytelling

Good conversation requires stories too.

Simple story structure:

setup → problem → reaction → ending

Example:

So I was getting on the train this morning, and the doors literally closed right in front of me. And I'm standing there like, seriously? Then I realized I'd left my laptop at home anyway, so I guess it worked out.

You don't need fancy vocabulary.

Part 73 — Storytelling Connectors

Useful:

  • So...
  • Anyway...
  • And then...
  • All of a sudden...
  • Turns out...
  • Apparently...
  • Basically...
  • Eventually...
  • In the end...
  • Next thing I know...

Example:

So we're sitting there waiting, and next thing I know, the lights go out. Turns out the whole building lost power.

Part 74 — Humor and Understatement

Spoken English often uses understatement.

After a terrible day:

Yeah, not my best day.

After a huge mistake:

That wasn't ideal.

After getting completely lost:

We took a slight detour.

This can sound more natural than dramatic language.

Part 75 — Sarcasm: Use Carefully

Example:

The printer breaks again.

Great. Perfect timing.

Meaning:

This is obviously not great.

Sarcasm is common, but tone matters.

If you're unsure, don't rely on it too much.

Part 76 — Softening

Softening

Definition: Making a statement less aggressive or absolute.

Compare:

That's wrong.

with:

I don't think that's quite right.

Compare:

This makes no sense.

with:

I'm not sure I follow the logic there.

Compare:

We can't do that.

with:

I don't think that's realistic right now.

Softening is very important in international environments.

Part 77 — Hedging

Hedging

Definition: Showing that a statement isn't completely certain or absolute.

Useful words:

  • probably
  • maybe
  • I think
  • it seems like
  • kind of
  • a bit
  • I'm not sure
  • from what I can tell

Example:

It seems like the main issue is pricing.

Different from:

The main issue is pricing.

Part 78 — Don't Over-Hedge

Too much:

I don't know, maybe, I guess it might possibly be a little too expensive.

Natural:

Yeah, I think it's probably too expensive.

Part 79 — Filler Words

Some fillers are natural.

  • well
  • so
  • like
  • I mean
  • you know
  • basically
  • actually
  • honestly

But don't put one in every sentence.

Bad:

So, like, basically, I mean, you know...

Better:

I mean, I get the idea. I just don't think it'll work.

Part 80 — Conversation Rhythm

Good spoken English alternates:

short → longer → short

Example:

Yeah, maybe. I just think if we change everything now, we're gonna create a bunch of problems we don't really need. So I'd leave it.

Not every sentence needs equal length.

Part 81 — Important Spoken Contractions

Natural speech uses contractions constantly.

  • I am → I'm
  • I would → I'd
  • I have → I've
  • we are → we're
  • they are → they're
  • do not → don't
  • cannot → can't
  • should not → shouldn't
  • would not → wouldn't
  • it is → it's
  • that is → that's

Writing:

I do not think we should do that.

Speech:

I don't think we should do that.

Part 82 — Gonna, Wanna, Kinda

You will hear:

  • gonna
  • wanna
  • gotta
  • kinda
  • sorta

You don't need to force them.

Natural:

I'm gonna head out.

I kinda like it.

Do you wanna grab lunch?

But in formal presentations, you may naturally pronounce words more fully.

Part 84 — One Idea Per Breath

Don't try to build enormous sentences.

Instead of:

I think we should probably consider delaying it because although the project is mostly finished there are still several things that could potentially cause problems once we launch.

Say:

I think we should probably delay it. It's mostly done, sure. But there are still a few things that could cause problems after launch.

Much easier to speak.

Much easier to understand.

Part 85 — Put Important Information Last

Compare:

Because we don't have enough time to properly test everything before Friday, I think we should delay the launch.

More conversational:

I don't think we should launch Friday. We just don't have enough time to test everything properly.

Conversation often gives the position early.

Part 86 — The Three-Second Answer

Practice answering quickly.

Question:

Did you like the movie?

Response:

Yeah, mostly. It was a little long, though.

Question:

Should we hire him?

I think so. He seems strong technically, and he came across really well.

Question:

Should we launch?

Not yet. I think we need another couple days.

You can expand afterward.

Part 87 — The Ten-Second Argument

Formula:

answer → one reason → consequence

Example:

I'd wait. We're still finding bugs, and if we rush it now, we're probably just creating more work for ourselves later.

Part 88 — The Thirty-Second Argument

Formula:

position → two reasons → qualification → conclusion

Example:

I'd probably keep the hybrid setup. People seem to like the flexibility, and we're still getting the work done. Sure, there are communication problems sometimes, but I don't think going fully back to the office automatically fixes those. So I'd keep it for now.

Still conversational.

Part 89 — Argument Map in Your Head

When speaking about something complicated, silently ask:

1. What am I saying?

Your conclusion.

2. Why?

Your strongest reason.

3. What supports that?

Evidence or example.

4. What might they say back?

Counterargument.

5. What's my response?

Rebuttal.

Then speak naturally.

Part 90 — Example of Full Conversational Reasoning

A: I think we should outsource the whole thing.

B: Maybe. I'm just worried we'll lose too much control.

A: But it'll be cheaper.

B: Yeah, probably. But cheaper doesn't necessarily mean better if we're spending half our time fixing things afterward.

A: So what would you do?

B: I'd outsource the repetitive stuff and keep the important parts in-house. At least to start with.

Logic:

  • outsourcing saves money
  • but control matters
  • low price may create downstream cost
  • split approach captures some benefit while reducing risk
  • conclusion: partial outsourcing

This is sophisticated reasoning in very normal English.

Part 91 — Difficult Personal Conversation

A: You've been pretty distant lately.

B: Yeah. I know.

A: Did I do something?

B: Not exactly. I've just been frustrated with how things have been going.

A: Like what?

B: I feel like whenever we make plans, I end up doing most of the work. And after a while, that gets tiring.

Notice:

  • no fancy words
  • clear example
  • focuses on behavior
  • explains effect

Part 92 — Telling Someone You're Upset

Avoid:

You never care about anything I say.

Try:

What bothered me was that I told you this mattered to me, and then it felt like you just ignored it.

Specific behavior is easier to discuss than global accusations.

Part 93 — When Someone Gets Defensive

That's not what I meant.

Response:

Okay, I believe you. I'm just telling you how it came across to me.

This separates:

intention from impact

Very useful distinction.

Part 94 — Apologizing

Simple:

Yeah, I'm sorry. I shouldn't have said that.

Better than:

I'm sorry if you were offended.

If you know what you did:

I was rude earlier. I'm sorry.

Short.

Clear.

Part 95 — Accepting an Apology

  • It's okay.
  • We're good.
  • I appreciate you saying that.
  • Thanks.
  • Yeah, let's move on.

If you're still upset:

I appreciate the apology. I just need a little time.

Part 96 — Setting a Boundary

Boundary

Definition: A limit on what you are willing to accept or do.

Examples:

I'm happy to help, but I can't keep taking this on at the last minute.

I don't mind talking about it, but I'm not gonna argue about it all night.

Please don't speak to me like that.

Strong boundaries can use simple language.

Part 97 — Saying You Need Time

I don't wanna answer right now. Let me think about it.

Can we come back to this tomorrow? I need a little time to think.

I'm not ready to make a decision yet.

Very useful.

Part 98 — Buying Time in a Meeting

Let me think about that for a second.

That's a good question.

Off the top of my head, I'd say...

I haven't looked at it from that angle.

Then answer.

Part 99 — When You Change Your Mind

Changing your mind is not weakness.

Say:

Actually, yeah, you might be right.

Okay, you've convinced me.

I hadn't thought about that.

Yeah, fair point. I take that back.

This makes conversation easier.

Part 100 — When Someone Makes a Good Point

Say it.

Yeah, that's actually a good point.

I hadn't thought of that.

Okay, that changes things a little.

Yeah, you're right about that part.

Good argumentation is not about refusing to move.

Part 101 — Persuasion

Persuasion works better when you connect your idea to the other person's concern.

Instead of:

We should delay.

Try:

I know you wanna avoid pushing the date again. I do too. I'm just worried that launching with these bugs is gonna create an even bigger delay later.

Rhetoric:

  • recognize their goal
  • show shared interest
  • explain your concern
  • connect it to their goal

Part 102 — Asking for Agreement

Instead of:

Do you agree with me?

Try:

  • Does that make sense?
  • How does that sound?
  • What do you think?
  • Would that work?
  • Is that fair?
  • Are you okay with that?

Part 103 — Negotiating

Useful structure:

need → constraint → alternative

I can get you the first version Friday. I just can't promise the full thing by then. If Monday works, I can get you everything.

Part 104 — Finding Middle Ground

Useful phrases:

  • What if we...
  • Could we meet halfway?
  • How about...
  • Maybe we do X for now.
  • Why don't we try it for a month?
  • Let's see how it goes.

Example:

What if we try two days in the office for a month and see how it goes?

Part 105 — Making Something Temporary

This lowers resistance.

We don't have to make it permanent.

Let's just try it for now.

We can always change it later.

Let's test it first.

Very powerful persuasive strategy.

Part 106 — Separating Facts From Opinions

Fact

Something you can verify.

Sales fell 12% last month.

Interpretation

What you think the fact means.

I think customers are reacting to the price increase.

Don't confuse them.

Natural version:

Sales are down 12%. My guess is the price increase has something to do with it, but we'd need to look into it.

Excellent reasoning.

Part 107 — Separating Problems From Solutions

Don't jump too quickly.

Before we decide what to do, can we make sure we understand what's actually causing the problem?

Extremely useful.

Part 108 — Separating Urgent From Important

Yeah, it's urgent, but I'm not sure it's actually the most important thing right now.

This is another logical distinction.

Part 109 — Separating Possible From Likely

Sure, it's possible. I just don't think it's very likely.

Excellent phrase.

Something being possible does not mean it is probable.

Part 110 — Separating Necessary From Nice to Have

Do we actually need this for launch, or would it just be nice to have?

Useful in work.

Part 111 — Separating Cause From Symptom

I think that's more of a symptom than the actual problem.

More conversational:

Yeah, but I think that's happening because of something else.

Example:

People being late isn't really the main problem. I think the bigger issue is that nobody knows who owns what.

Part 112 — Coffee-Talk Example: New Coworker

A: Hey, how are you settling in?

A: Yeah, this office is weirdly confusing.

A: That's normal. Took me a week.

Natural.

Simple.

Connected.

Part 116 — Challenging Conversation: Missed Deadline

A: Why isn't this finished?

Part 117 — Challenging Conversation: Unreasonable Request

Part 118 — Challenging Conversation: Rude Coworker

A: I'm just frustrated.

B: I get that. I'm frustrated too. Let's just focus on fixing it.

Strong.

Calm.

Simple.

Part 119 — Challenging Conversation: Credit for Your Work

A: We came up with this approach last week.

B: Just to be clear, that's actually the approach I proposed in Monday's meeting.

A: Yeah, but we developed it together.

B: Sure, we refined it together. I just want to make sure it's clear where the original idea came from.**

original idea vs later collaboration

Part 120 — Challenging Conversation: Being Pressured

A: Just say yes or no.

B: I can't give you a useful answer without knowing the budget.

A: Just give me your best guess.

B: My best guess is yes, but I wouldn't want us making the decision based on that.**

Excellent uncertainty management.

Part 121 — Challenging Conversation: You Strongly Disagree

A: I think we should fire the whole team and start over.

B: No, I think that's way too extreme.

A: Why?

B: Because we haven't even figured out whether the team is actually the problem. We'd be making a huge decision based on an assumption.**

Still logical.

Part 122 — A Natural Argument Toolkit

When you need to argue, reach for these:

Challenge the premise

Are we sure that's true?

Challenge the evidence

What are we basing that on?

Challenge the cause

How do we know that's what's causing it?

Challenge the conclusion

Does that necessarily mean we should do X?

Add an ignored factor

What about the cost?

Add a consequence

Okay, but what happens if that doesn't work?

Offer an alternative

Couldn't we just do X instead?

Find middle ground

What if we do part of it now?

Part 123 — The Best Simple Logic Words

You don't need academic connectors.

Use:

  • because
  • but
  • so
  • if
  • then
  • still
  • though
  • unless
  • even if
  • that's why
  • which means
  • otherwise

Examples:

If we wait, we lose time. But if we rush, we create more risk.

We can do it, but it'll cost more.

We need to decide today. Otherwise, we lose the reservation.

These tiny words carry huge amounts of logic.

Part 124 — Though at the End

Very conversational.

It's pretty expensive, though.

I liked it. A little long, though.

I don't think it'll happen. Could be wrong, though.

Part 125 — Actually

Useful for correction or contrast.

Actually, I think it's tomorrow.

I thought I'd hate it, but actually, it's pretty good.

Be careful not to use it before every disagreement.

Part 126 — Basically

Useful for simplifying.

Basically, we have two choices.

Basically, they want us to do the same work for less money.

Don't overuse it.

Part 127 — Honestly

Useful when giving a direct reaction.

Honestly, I wouldn't do it.

Honestly, I thought it was pretty good.

But you don't need it to prove you're telling the truth.

Part 128 — I Mean

Very important.

It can:

clarify

I mean, I'm not saying it's impossible.

soften

I mean, maybe we could try it.

continue thinking

I mean... I guess it depends.

correct

It's Thursday. I mean, Friday.

Very spoken.

Part 129 — The Thing Is

Excellent for introducing the real issue.

The thing is, we don't have the budget.

The thing is, I already told them we'd deliver Friday.

The thing is, even if we fix this, we still have the other problem.

Part 130 — Here's the Thing

Slightly stronger.

Here's the thing: we can keep changing the plan, but at some point we actually have to ship something.

Good before a central argument.

Part 131 — Here's My Problem With That

Useful, but stronger.

Here's my problem with that. We're assuming customers care about this feature, and I don't think we actually know that.

Part 132 — I Just Think

Very useful softener.

I just think we're moving too fast.

I just think there has to be a better way.

It makes an opinion sound less aggressive.

Part 133 — At the Same Time

Useful when two things are true.

I understand why they want it quickly. At the same time, we can't promise something we know we can't deliver.

This is balanced reasoning.

Part 134 — On the Other Hand

A little more formal, but still common.

Working from home is convenient. On the other hand, you do miss some of the social side.

Part 135 — Then Again

More conversational.

I could go tonight. Then again, I'm pretty tired.

Shows reconsideration.

Part 136 — Fair Enough

Means:

I accept that point.

A: I can't come. I've got an early flight.

B: Fair enough.**

Can also end a minor disagreement.

Part 137 — That's Fair

Useful when recognizing criticism or reasoning.

Yeah, that's fair. I probably should've told you earlier.

Part 138 — I See Your Point

Useful, but slightly more polished.

More conversational:

Yeah, I see what you mean.

Part 139 — That's Not Really My Point

Useful when misunderstood.

That's not really my point. I'm not saying we shouldn't invest. I'm saying we shouldn't invest everything at once.

Part 140 — You're Missing My Point

Stronger.

Use carefully.

Softer:

I think we're slightly missing each other here.

Part 141 — That's Not What I'm Saying

Very useful.

I'm not saying nobody should come into the office. That's not what I'm saying. I'm saying people don't need to be here every single day.

Part 142 — That's Different

Useful for distinguishing cases.

Yeah, but that's different. That project had six months. We've got three weeks.

Logic often depends on whether two situations are actually comparable.

Part 143 — That's Not the Same Thing

Strong but useful.

Wanting more information isn't the same thing as refusing to make a decision.

Great logical structure:

X ≠ Y

Part 144 — It Depends

Don't stop there.

Weak:

It depends.

Better:

It depends on the cost. If it's under ten thousand, I'd probably do it. If it's much more than that, I'm not sure.

Always explain:

depends on what?

Part 145 — Maybe

Maybe is powerful.

Maybe.

can mean:

  • I'm unconvinced.
  • I'm thinking.
  • I partly agree.
  • I don't want to reject you directly.

Example:

Part 146 — Probably

Good for practical judgments.

Part 148 — Conversation Is Collaborative

Then react.

Conversation is built together.

Part 149 — Listen for the Other Person's Logic

When they speak, mentally identify:

  • What are they claiming?
  • Why do they believe it?
  • What are they assuming?
  • What are they worried about?
  • What do they actually want?

Then respond to the strongest part.

Part 150 — Don't Attack the Weakest Version

If someone says:

I'm worried remote work is hurting communication.

Don't answer:

So you think everyone should sit in an office all day?

They didn't say that.

Better:

Yeah, I can see that. Do you think the problem is remote work itself, or just how we're communicating?

That is stronger reasoning.

Part 151 — Ask What Would Change Their Mind

Useful in serious disagreement:

What would you need to see to feel comfortable with it?

What would change your mind?

What would make this work for you?

This moves conversation forward.

Part 152 — State What Would Change Your Mind

If the data shows customers actually want it, I'm happy to change my view.

If we can do it without delaying launch, then I'm fine with it.

This makes you sound reasonable rather than stubborn.

Part 153 — The Core Mental Model

Before you speak, think:

What happened?

What do I think?

Why?

What matters most?

What does the other person think?

Where exactly do we disagree?

What should happen next?

You don't need to say all of this.

It organizes your response.

Part 154 — Daily Practice: 5 Minutes

Pick one topic.

Examples:

  • working from home
  • AI
  • commuting
  • expensive restaurants
  • social media
  • a movie
  • weekend plans
  • office meetings
  • learning languages
  • exercise

Then answer:

Round 1

Speak for 20 seconds.

Round 2

Give one reason.

Round 3

Give an example.

Round 4

Imagine someone disagrees.

Round 5

Respond to them.

Part 155 — Daily Practice: Coffee Talk

Pick one ordinary topic.

The weather is terrible.

Build a conversation:

Yeah, seriously. I thought it was supposed to clear up today.

Then imagine:

Apparently it's like this all week.

Respond:

Great. Guess summer's over.

Keep going for ten turns.

Part 156 — Daily Practice: Difficult Conversation

Scenario:

Your manager gives you an impossible deadline.

Practice:

I don't think Friday is realistic.

Then imagine:

It has to be Friday.

Respond:

Okay. Then we'd need to cut some of the scope.

Then:

We can't cut anything.

Respond:

Then I don't think I can promise Friday. I don't wanna tell you yes now and miss it later.

This is excellent speaking practice.

Part 157 — Logic Drill

Take a claim:

We should cancel the project.

Ask:

  • Why?
  • What evidence?
  • What assumption?
  • What alternative?
  • What downside?
  • What would change my mind?

Then turn the answers into spoken English.

Part 158 — One-Minute Conversation Training

Do not write a speech first.

Give yourself only three notes:

position

reason

example

Then speak.

This prevents written English from taking over.

Part 159 — Record Yourself

Listen for:

  • Are my sentences too long?
  • Do I sound like I'm reading?
  • Do I use normal contractions?
  • Am I giving reasons?
  • Do I respond to the other person's point?
  • Do I leave space?
  • Do I repeat the same phrases?
  • Can I make the same point more simply?

Part 160 — The Simplification Test

After saying something, ask:

Could I say this in half the words?

Formal:

I have some concerns regarding the viability of this approach.

Natural:

I'm not sure this is gonna work.

Formal:

I would prefer to explore alternative options.

Natural:

I'd look at a few other options first.

Formal:

We need to determine the underlying cause.

Natural:

We need to figure out what's actually causing it.

Part 161 — The Movie-Script Test

Ask yourself:

Would a normal character in a modern movie actually say this out loud?

If not, simplify it.

Instead of:

I strongly believe that communication is an essential component of successful teamwork.

Try:

I mean, if people aren't talking to each other, the team's obviously gonna struggle.

Part 162 — Do Not Chase Perfect Grammar

Your priority order should usually be:

  1. clear thought
  2. understandable sentence
  3. natural rhythm
  4. correct grammar
  5. sophisticated vocabulary

Not:

  1. impressive vocabulary
  2. perfect grammar
  3. everything else

Part 164 — The Golden Rule of Conversational Logic

Keep the reasoning structured.

Keep the language loose.

Think:

premise → premise → conclusion

Speak:

Yeah, I don't know. We're already pretty busy, and this is gonna add a lot more work. I'd probably wait.

Think:

concession → objection → alternative

Speak:

Yeah, that's true. I just don't think another meeting fixes it. Why don't we try a shared update instead?

Think:

claim → uncertainty → evidence → conclusion

Speak:

I think pricing might be the problem. We've had a bunch of people drop off right after they see the plans. I'd look into that first.

Final Principle

You are not trying to sound impressive.

You are trying to become someone who can enter almost any conversation and do four things:

  • understand what is really being said
  • form a clear thought quickly
  • support that thought with reasons
  • express it in ordinary, natural English

That means your ideal spoken English often sounds almost boring on paper:

Yeah, maybe.

I don't know about that.

The thing is...

That's true, but...

What if we just...

I don't think that's the real problem.

So I'd probably leave it for now.

But underneath those simple sentences can be excellent logic, careful judgment, persuasion, disagreement, negotiation, humor, empathy, and confidence.

Simple mouth. Structured mind.

That's the target.
